Hypotheses and formulas

H1: The tested alleged father is the biological father of the child, with the tested mother being the biological mother.

H2: The tested mother is the biological mother, and an unrelated random man from the specified allele-frequency population is the biological father.

For each locus, the numerator is P(child genotype | mother genotype, alleged-father genotype, H1). The denominator is P(child genotype | mother genotype, H2). The locus LR = numerator / denominator. CPI is the product of the locus LRs.

The engine enumerates maternal and paternal gametes. Under H2, the random father's transmitted allele is weighted by its population allele frequency. This handles ambiguous maternal transmission without arbitrarily selecting one paternal allele.

Frequency provenance warning: the supplied JSON does not identify its reference population or validation source. Confirm population relevance and source documentation before interpreting results.

Current assumptions: autosomal codominant STRs; Mendelian transmission; Hardy–Weinberg/random-mating allele-frequency model; no mutation; no null alleles; no genotyping error; no population-substructure correction (θ = 0); alleged father unrelated to the mother and to the alternative father. No related-father alternative is modelled.

Allele frequencies are sourced from the supplied STR Allele Probability Database JSON. Its reference population is not identified in the supplied file. Missing allele frequencies are flagged; they are not invented or silently normalized.

Understand the Paternity Index, one allele at a time.

Follow the inheritance of STR alleles, learn what the frequency means, and see how a locus Paternity Index becomes a Combined Paternity Index (CPI).

1. Start with inheritance

A child receives one allele at each autosomal STR locus from each biological parent.

Illustrative inheritance: child genotype 14, 15. The paternal allele is the allele that cannot be explained by the mother's genotype.

2. Identify the obligate paternal allele

Compare the mother and child first, then check whether the alleged father carries the inferred paternal allele.

A

Mother: 12, 14

These are the two alleles the mother could have passed to the child.

B

Child: 14, 15

Allele 14 can be maternal. Allele 15 is not present in the mother, so it is the obligate paternal allele in this example.

C

Alleged father: 15, 17

The alleged father carries allele 15. This is compatible with transmission at this locus; one locus alone does not establish paternity.

Remember: If both child alleles are present in the mother, the paternal contribution may be ambiguous. Additional interpretation or a clearly supported obligate allele selection is needed. Do not force a choice just to obtain a number.

3. Understand the Paternity Index

The PI compares the likelihood of the observed genetic evidence under competing paternity hypotheses.

PIPaternity Index at one locus
=
LRLikelihood ratio
÷
pRelevant allele frequency

This calculator's displayed locus calculation uses its configured numerator (1 for a homozygous alleged father, 0.5 for a heterozygous alleged father) divided by the selected obligate paternal allele frequency. This is the model implemented in this workbook-style calculator.

What is allele frequency?

It is the proportion of a particular allele in the reference population database. For example, 0.10 means 10% on the frequency scale.

Why does rarity matter?

A lower frequency generally gives a larger index in this calculator's formula, because the denominator is smaller.

What does PI mean?

A locus PI is a likelihood ratio for the genetic evidence at that locus under the stated model. It is not a direct probability that the alleged father is the father.

4. Watch a worked calculation

Example values are illustrative. Try changing the allele frequency and numerator.

Example: heterozygous alleged father → numerator 0.5; selected allele frequency → 0.10.

PI = numerator ÷ frequency
5
0.5 ÷ 0.10 = 5. A PI of 5 means the observed locus evidence is five times as likely under H1 as under H2, within the assumptions of the model.
